\section{Introduction}

Information theory provides the mathematical framework for quantifying information and establishing fundamental limits on data compression. A central pillar of this field is Shannon's Source Coding Theorem, which establishes the entropy $H(X)$ as the lower bound for the average code length of an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) source. While i.i.d. models are useful first approximations, real-world data sources---such as natural language---exhibit strong temporal dependencies (memory) and non-uniform distributions.

This report extends the investigation of the 3F7 short laboratory in two specific directions. First, we analyse \textbf{discrete stationary sources}, relaxing the i.i.d. assumption to model sources with memory. We investigate the \textit{entropy rate} $\Hrate$ and the convergence properties of block entropies. Second, we explore \textbf{adaptive coding}, where the probability model is learned ``on the fly.'' We derive the Laplace estimator from Bayesian principles and evaluate the performance of adaptive arithmetic coding against static methods.

\section{Overview of Short Lab}

The short laboratory focused on compressing \texttt{hamlet.txt} (approx. 207kB) using three classical techniques designed for i.i.d. sources.

\textbf{Shannon-Fano Coding} constructs prefix-free codes by assigning lengths $l_i = \lceil \log_2(1/p_i) \rceil$. While it guarantees unique decodability via the Kraft inequality $\sum 2^{-l_i} \le 1$, it is suboptimal because it ignores the combinatorial nature of packing the code tree.

\textbf{Huffman Coding} is the optimal symbol-by-symbol prefix code. It builds a binary tree bottom-up by iteratively merging the two least probable symbols. Our experiments confirmed that its expected length $L$ satisfies $H(X) \le L < H(X) + 1$. However, its performance is bounded by the requirement that each symbol must use an integer number of bits.

\textbf{Arithmetic Coding} overcomes the integer constraint by encoding entire sequences as sub-intervals of $[0, 1)$. By mapping a sequence $x^n$ to an interval of width $P(x^n)$, it achieves a code length $L \approx -\log_2 P(x^n)$ bits. For large $n$, the per-symbol length converges to the entropy $H(X)$. This method proved superior for longer blocks but required accurate probability models. The limitation of all three methods in the short lab was the assumption that text is a memoryless source, ignoring the significant redundancy in English grammar and spelling.

\section{Coding for Discrete Stationary Sources}

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

A discrete stochastic process $\mathcal{X} = \{X_i\}_{i=1}^\infty$ is \textbf{strictly stationary} if the joint distribution of any subsequence is invariant to time shifts. That is, for any shift $\tau$ and block length $n$:
\begin{equation}
    \Prob(X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_n = x_n) = \Prob(X_{1+\tau} = x_1, \dots, X_{n+\tau} = x_n).
\end{equation}
Stationarity implies that marginal probabilities are constant ($H(X_i) = H(X_1)$) and conditional probabilities depend only on the relative history. We define two entropy sequences to characterize such sources:
\begin{align}
    H_n   & = \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, \dots, X_n) \quad \text{(Entropy per symbol of $n$-block)} \\
    H_n^* & = H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \dots, X_1) \quad \text{(Conditional entropy)}
\end{align}

\subsection{Convergence of Entropy Rates (Theorem 1)}

For stationary sources, these sequences converge to the \textit{entropy rate} $\Hrate$. We provide proofs for the properties stated in the guidance, referencing Cover \& Thomas.

\textbf{Statement 1: $H_{n+1}^* \le H_n^*$} (Conditional entropy is non-increasing). \\
\textit{Proof:}
\begin{align*}
    H_{n+1}^* & = H(X_{n+1} | X_n, \dots, X_1)                                               \\
              & = H(X_{n+1} | X_n, \dots, X_2, X_1)                                          \\
              & \le H(X_{n+1} | X_n, \dots, X_2) \quad \text{(Conditioning reduces entropy)} \\
              & = H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \dots, X_1) \quad \text{(By Stationarity)}                \\
              & = H_n^* \quad \square
\end{align*}

\textbf{Statement 2: $H_{n+1} \le H_n$} (Per-symbol entropy is non-increasing). \\
\textit{Proof:} By the chain rule, $H(X_1, \dots, X_{n+1}) = H(X_1, \dots, X_n) + H(X_{n+1} | X^n)$.
Dividing by $n+1$:
\begin{equation}
    H_{n+1} = \frac{n H_n + H_{n+1}^*}{n+1}.
\end{equation}
Since $H_{n+1}^* \le H_n^*$ (from Statement 1) and $H_n$ is the average of the first $n$ conditional entropies (see Statement 3 proof), adding a term $H_{n+1}^*$ that is smaller than or equal to the previous average must reduce (or maintain) the new average. Thus $H_{n+1} \le H_n$. $\square$

\textbf{Statement 3: $H_n^* \le H_n$.} \\
\textit{Proof:} From the Chain Rule for entropy,
\begin{equation}
    H(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n H(X_i | X_{i-1}, \dots, X_1).
\end{equation}
Dividing by $n$:
\begin{equation}
    H_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n H_i^*.
\end{equation}
Since $H_i^*$ is a non-increasing sequence (Statement 1), the current term $H_n^*$ is less than or equal to all preceding terms $H_i^*$ for $i < n$. Therefore, the average $H_n$ must be greater than or equal to the minimum term $H_n^*$. $\square$

\textbf{Statement 4: $\lim_{n \to \infty} H_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} H_n^* = \Hrate$.} \\
\textit{Proof:} Since $H_n^*$ is non-increasing (Statement 1) and bounded below by 0 (entropy is non-negative), the limit $\lim_{n \to \infty} H_n^* = H'$ exists. Since $H_n$ is the arithmetic mean (Cesàro mean) of the sequence $H_n^*$, by the \textbf{Cesàro Mean Theorem} (if $a_n \to a$, then $\frac{1}{n}\sum a_i \to a$), the sequence of averages converges to the same limit. This limit is defined as the entropy rate $\Hrate$. $\square$

\subsection{Experimental Results: The Sparse Data Problem}

We measured $H_n$ and $H_n^*$ for \texttt{hamlet.txt} up to $n=15$ (Fig. \ref{fig:entropy_rate}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item For small $n$ ($n=1, 2, 3$), $H_n$ decreases ($4.2 \to 3.7 \to \dots$), confirming the source has memory.
    \item For large $n$, the estimates plunge towards 0.
\end{itemize}
This decay to zero is an artifact of \textbf{undersampling}. The number of possible sequences $|\mathcal{X}|^n$ grows exponentially. With $|\mathcal{X}| \approx 70$ and $N \approx 200,000$ symbols, for $n \ge 4$, the vast majority of observed $n$-tuples occur only once. The Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) for entropy on unique samples is $\log(N)/n$, which vanishes as $n$ grows. This illustrates that empirical measurement of $\Hrate$ is impossible without a model or exponentially more data.

\section{Adaptive Coding}

\subsection{Bayesian Derivation of the Laplace Estimator}

Standard coding assumes a fixed distribution $P(x)$. In adaptive coding, we estimate $P(x)$ sequentially. Consider a binary source where the probability of a symbol being '1' is $\theta$. We model our uncertainty about $\theta$ with a \textbf{Beta prior} $Beta(\alpha, \beta)$, which acts as a "conjugate prior" for the Bernoulli distribution.

The prior probability density function is:
\begin{equation}
    P(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha-1} (1-\theta)^{\beta-1}
\end{equation}
Starting with a uniform prior $Beta(1, 1)$ (representing total ignorance, $\alpha=\beta=1$), and observing data $D$ consisting of $n_0$ zeros and $n_1$ ones, the posterior distribution is:
\begin{equation}
    P(\theta | D) \propto \theta^{n_1 + \alpha - 1} (1-\theta)^{n_0 + \beta - 1} = \theta^{n_1} (1-\theta)^{n_0}
\end{equation}
which is a $Beta(n_1+1, n_0+1)$ distribution. The predictive probability for the next symbol being '1' is the expected value of $\theta$ under this posterior:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta} = \E[\theta | D] = \int_0^1 \theta P(\theta|D) d\theta = \frac{n_1 + 1}{(n_1+1) + (n_0+1)} = \frac{n_1+1}{n+2}.
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Laplace Estimator}, also known as the "Rule of Succession". Generalizing to an alphabet of size $K$ with counts $n_x$ for each symbol $x$, this becomes the Dirichlet-Multinomial estimator:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}(x) = \frac{n_x + \delta}{n + K\delta}
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the bias parameter. $\delta=1$ corresponds to the uniform prior.

\subsection{Experimental Performance}

We implemented an adaptive arithmetic coder using this estimator.

\textbf{Convergence:} Fig. \ref{fig:convergence} shows the running average code length $L/n$ converging to the static entropy $H_1$. The "learning penalty" (excess code length) decreases as $O(1/n)$, consistent with universal coding theory.

\textbf{Bias Parameter $\delta$:} We varied $\delta$ (Fig. \ref{fig:delta}) and found the optimal range $\delta \in [0.5, 1.0]$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small $\delta$ ($< 0.1$): Overfitting. Unseen symbols have near-zero probability, causing massive penalties.
    \item Large $\delta$ ($> 5$): Underfitting. The strong prior washes out the observed data, slowing adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Adaptive vs. Non-Adaptive:} Fig. \ref{fig:comparison} compares total bits. Non-adaptive coding requires overhead to store the frequency table ($\approx K \log n$ bits). Adaptive coding has no header but higher initial redundancy. The crossover point for Hamlet is at $n \approx 50,000$ symbols. For files shorter than this, adaptive coding is more efficient.

\section{Conclusion}

This report demonstrated that while English text is a stationary source with memory, exploiting this theoretically (via $\Hrate$) is hindered by sparse data. Adaptive coding offers a robust solution, allowing compression to approach the source entropy without prior knowledge of statistics. By deriving the Laplace estimator from Bayesian principles, we showed that adding a "pseudocount" of 1 is not heuristic but the optimal strategy under a uniform prior assumption. This principle underpins modern compressors like LZ77 and prediction models in Large Language Models.
