\noindent \textbf{Question 1.} \textit{(§2.2 Modelling Manual Control) Nyquist diagram (from Bode diagram) for controller in series with plant.}

\textbf{System Model:} The simplified aircraft has transfer function $G(s) = \dfrac{N}{s^2 + Ms} = \dfrac{10}{s(s+10)}$, representing an integrator ($1/s$) in series with a first-order lag. The human controller is modelled as $K(s) = ke^{-sD}$ with proportional gain $k=0.8$ and reaction delay $D=1$s.

\textbf{Open-Loop Transfer Function:}
$$ L(s) = K(s)G(s) = \frac{8 e^{-s}}{s(s + 10)} $$

\textbf{Frequency Response:} Substituting $s = j\omega$:
$$ L(j\omega) = \frac{8 e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega(j\omega + 10)} = \frac{8}{j\omega \cdot \sqrt{\omega^2+100} \cdot e^{j\arctan(\omega/10)}} \cdot e^{-j\omega} $$

The magnitude and phase are:
$$ |L(j\omega)| = \frac{8}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+100}}, \quad \angle L(j\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan\left(\frac{\omega}{10}\right) - \omega $$

\textbf{Key Points from Bode Diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:bode_manual}):}
\begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Low frequency} ($\omega \to 0$): $|L| \to \infty$, $\angle L \to -90^\circ$ (integrator dominates).
        \item \textbf{Gain crossover} ($\omega_{gc} \approx 0.8$ rad/s): $|L|=1$ (0 dB). Phase at this point:
              $$ \angle L(j \cdot 0.8) = -90^\circ - \arctan(0.08) - 0.8 \times \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx -90^\circ - 4.6^\circ - 45.8^\circ \approx -140^\circ $$
              Phase margin $= 180^\circ - 140^\circ = 40^\circ$ (approximately $45^\circ$ from graph).
        \item \textbf{High frequency} ($\omega \to \infty$): $|L| \to 0$, $\angle L \to -\infty$ (time delay adds unbounded phase lag $-\omega$ rad).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Constructing Nyquist from Bode:} The Nyquist plot traces $L(e^{j\theta})$ as $\omega$ varies from $0$ to $\infty$. Starting from the negative imaginary axis (phase $-90^\circ$, large magnitude), the locus spirals clockwise towards the origin as magnitude decreases and phase becomes increasingly negative. The critical point $-1$ is not encircled, confirming closed-loop stability.

\noindent \textbf{2.} (§2.2 Modelling Manual Control) Are you using any integral action?

Yes. In Figure~\ref{fig:step_manual}, the manual control input $u(t)$ does not return to zero but maintains a steady value to counteract the step disturbance, while $e(t) \to 0$. This implies the human controller provides integral action (``trimming'') to eliminate steady-state error. The simple model $K(s) = ke^{-sD}$ is therefore an approximation that neglects the pilot's low-frequency adaptation.

\noindent \textbf{3.} (§2.3 Pilot Induced Oscillation) Explain the oscillation of the feedback loop.

\textbf{Mechanism:} PIO occurs when the pilot's time delay introduces sufficient phase lag that the total loop phase reaches $-180^\circ$ at a frequency where the loop gain exceeds unity. The PIO aircraft has $G_1(s) = c/(Ts+1)^3$ which contributes up to $-270^\circ$ phase lag, plus the pilot delay $e^{-sD}$ adds $-\omega D$ radians.

\textbf{Stability condition:} At the phase crossover frequency $\omega_{pc}$ where $\angle L(j\omega_{pc}) = -180^\circ$, if $|L(j\omega_{pc})| > 1$, the system is unstable and oscillates at frequency $\omega_{pc}$.

\textbf{Period comparison:} From Bode (Figure~\ref{fig:pio_bode}), $\omega_{pc} \approx 0.8$ rad/s, giving theoretical period $T = 2\pi/\omega_{pc} \approx 7.85$s. The observed period ($\approx 5$s) is shorter, suggesting the pilot adapts their gain/delay under oscillatory conditions.

\noindent \textbf{4.} (§2.3 Pilot Induced Oscillation) Can you give a rough guideline to the control designer to make PIO less likely?

To prevent PIO, ensure adequate phase margin at frequencies where the pilot is active ($< 1$ Hz):
\begin{itemize}
        \item Reduce total system delay (sensors, actuators, displays)
        \item Avoid plants with high phase lag at low frequencies
        \item Design for phase margin $> 45^\circ$ at the expected pilot crossover frequency
        \item Consider rate limiters to prevent high-frequency pilot inputs
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{5.} (§2.4 Sinusoidal Disturbances) Was your manual input able to reduce the error?

No. The closed-loop response to disturbance $d$ is $y = \dfrac{G}{1+KG} d$. For disturbance rejection, we need $|1+KG| > 1$.

At 0.66 Hz ($\omega = 4.15$ rad/s), pilot delay adds phase $-\omega D = -4.15 \times 1 \approx -238^\circ$. Combined with plant phase ($\approx -90^\circ$ from Bode), total phase approaches $-330^\circ$, meaning $KG$ points nearly opposite to $-1$. This makes $|1+KG| < 1$, so the disturbance is actually \emph{amplified} rather than attenuated. Manual control worsens the oscillation.

\noindent \textbf{6.} (§2.5 An Unstable Aircraft) Nyquist diagram for $G_2(s).$

$G_2(s) = \dfrac{2}{sT-1}$ has one unstable pole at $s=1/T > 0$. The frequency response is:
$$ G_2(j\omega) = \frac{2}{j\omega T - 1} = \frac{-2(1 + j\omega T)}{1 + \omega^2 T^2} $$

At $\omega = 0$: $G_2(0) = -2$. As $\omega \to \infty$: $G_2 \to 0$. The locus is a circle centered at $(-1, 0)$ with radius 1, traced clockwise from $-2$ to $0$.

\noindent \textbf{7.} (§2.5 An Unstable Aircraft) Explain, using the Nyquist criterion, why the feedback system is stable with a proportional gain greater than 0.5.

\textbf{Nyquist Criterion:} For a system with $P$ open-loop poles in the RHP, closed-loop stability requires $N = -P$ encirclements of $-1/K$, where $N > 0$ is counter-clockwise.

Here $P = 1$ (one unstable pole), so we need $N = -1$: one \emph{clockwise} encirclement of $-1/K$.

The Nyquist locus is a circle from $-2$ to $0$ on the real axis, traversed clockwise. For $-1/K$ to be encircled clockwise:
$$ -2 < -\frac{1}{K} < 0 \implies 0 < \frac{1}{K} < 2 \implies K > 0.5 $$

For $K > 0.5$, the point $-1/K$ lies inside the circle, giving the required clockwise encirclement for stability.

\noindent \textbf{8.} (§2.5 An Unstable Aircraft) Sketch of a Nyquist diagram for $G_2(s)$ with a small time delay $D$.

With delay, the transfer function becomes $G_2(s)e^{-sD}$. The delay contributes phase $-\omega D$ without changing magnitude:
$$ |G_2(j\omega)e^{-j\omega D}| = |G_2(j\omega)|, \quad \angle(G_2 e^{-j\omega D}) = \angle G_2 - \omega D $$

The additional phase lag causes the circular locus to spiral inward rather than close cleanly at the origin. For large $D$, the spiral may cross $-1/K$ multiple times, potentially destabilizing the system.

\noindent \textbf{9.} (§3.4 Integrator Wind-up) Explain how you calculated the bound on $Q$.

\textbf{PID Controller:} $u(t) = K_p \left( e + \dfrac{1}{T_i}\displaystyle\int_0^t e \, d\tau + T_d \dfrac{de}{dt} \right)$

\textbf{Steady-state analysis:} After settling, $e_{ss} = 0$ and $\dot{e} = 0$. To cancel a step disturbance $d$, we need $u_{ss} = d$. Only the integral term contributes:
$$ u_{ss} = \frac{K_p}{T_i} I_{ss} = d \implies I_{ss} = \frac{d \cdot T_i}{K_p} $$

\textbf{Minimum bound:} For $d = 2$, $T_i = 1.0$, $K_p = 7.2$:
$$ Q = \frac{2 \times 1.0}{7.2} = \frac{5}{18} \approx 0.278 $$

Setting $Q$ smaller would prevent full disturbance rejection (steady-state error). Setting $Q$ larger allows more wind-up overshoot.
