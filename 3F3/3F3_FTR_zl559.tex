\section{Introduction}

Random number generation underpins modern computational statistics. While pseudorandom number generators readily produce uniform variates, most applications require samples from complex probability distributions. This report develops practical techniques for generating and analyzing non-uniform random variables.

We begin by examining density estimation methods, quantifying histogram uncertainty and how kernel methods achieve better convergence. Probability transformations demonstrate how complex distributions arise from simple ones through functional relationships. The inverse CDF method provides a general framework for sampling arbitrary distributions. Finally, we characterize $\alpha$-stable distributions, whose heavy tails model phenomena beyond Gaussian assumptions.

\section{Uniform and Normal Random Variables}
\label{sec:uniform_normal}

This section compares histogram and kernel density estimation methods, using multinomial distribution theory to quantify histogram uncertainty.

\subsection{Kernel Density Estimation}

The kernel density estimator is defined as
\begin{equation}
	\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{\sigma}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{x - x^{(i)}}{\sigma}\right)
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of samples, $x^{(i)}$ are the observed data points, $\mathcal{K}(u)$ is the kernel function, and $\sigma$ is the bandwidth. The Gaussian kernel is:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{K}_{\text{Gauss}}(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\right)
\end{equation}
resulting in the KDE:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{N\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp\left(-\frac{(x - x^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Comparison of Methods}

Both methods have similar bias-variance structure but differ in asymptotic convergence. From statistical theory \autocite{UW_STAT425}: Histogram has an optimal convergence rate of $O(N^{-2/3})$ whereas KDE has an optimal convergence rate of $O(N^{-4/5})$ (faster).

\textbf{KDE disadvantages:} Less intuitive, boundary effects for finite support distributions, computationally more expensive.

\subsection{Multinomial Distribution}

The multinomial distribution describes the probability of observing counts $(n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_J)$ when $N$ independent samples are distributed into $J$ bins, where each sample falls into bin $j$ with probability $p_j$:
\begin{equation}
	P(n_1, \ldots, n_J) = \frac{N!}{n_1! n_2! \cdots n_J!} p_1^{n_1} p_2^{n_2} \cdots p_J^{n_J}
	\label{eq:multinomial}
\end{equation}

Each sample is an independent trial. For a specific sample to land in bin $j$, the probability is $p_j$. If exactly $n_j$ samples land in bin $j$, these contribute a factor of $p_j^{n_j}$. Since all $N$ samples are independent, the probability of a specific sequence of outcomes is the product of individual probabilities $p_1^{n_1} \times p_2^{n_2} \times \cdots \times p_J^{n_J}$.

The factorial coefficient counts the number of distinct arrangements that yield the same bin counts. Consider $N$ labelled samples: the number of ways to choose which $n_1$ samples go to bin 1, then which $n_2$ of the remaining go to bin 2, and so forth, is:
\begin{equation}
	\binom{N}{n_1}\binom{N-n_1}{n_2}\cdots\binom{n_J}{n_J} = \frac{N!}{n_1! n_2! \cdots n_J!}
\end{equation}
This multinomial coefficient counts the number of ways to partition $N$ distinct objects into $J$ groups of sizes $n_1, \ldots, n_J$. Equation~\eqref{eq:multinomial} thus gives the probability of observing a specific pattern of bin counts.

\subsection{Multinomial Analysis of Uniform Distribution}

When histogramming $N$ samples from $\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ into $J$ bins, each bin has width $\delta = 1/J$. The probability that a sample falls in bin $j$ is:
\begin{equation}
	p_j = \int_{(j-1)/J}^{j/J} p(x)\,dx = \int_{(j-1)/J}^{j/J} 1\,dx = \frac{1}{J}
\end{equation}

Each marginal count follows a binomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
	n_j \sim \text{Binomial}\left(N, p_j = \frac{1}{J}\right)
\end{equation}

Using the binomial mean formula $E[X] = Np$:
\begin{equation}
	E[n_j] = N \cdot p_j = N \cdot \frac{1}{J} = \frac{N}{J}
\end{equation}

Using the binomial variance formula $\text{Var}(X) = Np(1-p)$:
\begin{align}
	\text{Var}(n_j) & = N \cdot p_j \cdot (1 - p_j) = \frac{N(J-1)}{J^2}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{n_j} = \sqrt{\text{Var}(n_j)} = \sqrt{\frac{N(J-1)}{J^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{N(J-1)}}{J}
\end{equation}

The coefficient of variation (relative uncertainty) is:
\begin{align}
	\text{CV} & = \frac{\sigma_{n_j}}{E[n_j]} = \frac{\sqrt{N(J-1)}/J}{N/J} \nonumber       \\
	          & = \frac{\sqrt{N(J-1)}}{N} = \sqrt{\frac{J-1}{N}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{J}{N}}
\end{align}

As $N \to \infty$ with $J$ fixed, $\text{CV} \sim 1/\sqrt{N} \to 0$, so histogram estimates become increasingly reliable. By the Law of Large Numbers, observed bin counts $n_j/N \to p_j = 1/J$.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multinomial Analysis for Gaussian (Normal) Distribution}

For normally distributed data $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, the bin probabilities $p_j$ are non-uniform. They must be calculated using the CDF:
\begin{equation}
	p_j = \Phi(b_{j+1}) - \Phi(b_j)
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(x)$ is the standard normal CDF and $[b_j, b_{j+1})$ defines bin $j$. Central bins (near $x = 0$) have high $p_j$ because the PDF is peaked there, while edge bins (large $|x|$) have low $p_j$ due to exponential decay.

Each bin count still follows a binomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
	n_j \sim \text{Binomial}(N, p_j)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	E[n_j] = N p_j
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\text{Var}(n_j) = N p_j (1 - p_j)
\end{equation}
This is maximised when $p_j = 0.5$ and minimised when $p_j \to 0$ or $p_j \to 1$.

\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{n_j} = \sqrt{N p_j (1 - p_j)}
\end{equation}

\begin{align}
	\text{CV}_j & = \frac{\sigma_{n_j}}{E[n_j]} = \frac{\sqrt{N p_j (1 - p_j)}}{N p_j} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - p_j}{N p_j}}
\end{align}

For edge bins where $p_j \to 0$, we have $(1-p_j) \to 1$, so $\text{CV}_j \approx 1/\sqrt{Np_j} \to \infty$. Thus edge bins have the highest relative uncertainty, making histogram estimates in the tails less reliable. Table~\ref{tab:normal_variance} illustrates this for bins at 0, 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations from the mean with $N = 1000$ samples. Although absolute variance decreases in the tails, the coefficient of variation increases from 0.07 at the centre to 0.73 at 3 standard deviations.

\section{Functions of Random Variables}
\label{sec:functions}

Random variable transformations reveal how probability distributions change under functional mappings. Using the Jacobian formula, we derive output distributions for linear, quadratic, and sinusoidal transformations.

\subsection{Linear Transformation}

For $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $y = ax + b$, the inverse is $x = (y-b)/a$ with Jacobian $|dx/dy| = 1/|a|$. Applying the transformation formula:
\begin{align}
	p(y) & = p(x)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| \nonumber                                                   \\
	     & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-b)^2}{2a^2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{|a|}  \nonumber \\
	     & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-b)^2}{2a^2}\right) \nonumber                  \\
	     & = \mathcal{N}(y|b, a^2)
\end{align}

This demonstrates that a linear transformation of standard normal produces $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\mu = b$ and $\sigma^2 = a^2$. Any normal distribution can be generated from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ using $Y = \sigma X + \mu$. Figure~\ref{fig:linear} verifies this result empirically.

\begin{figure}[h] % Single column figure
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/linear_transform_verification.png}
	\caption{Verification of linear transformation $y = 3x + 5$ where $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The histogram matches theoretical $\mathcal{N}(5, 9)$.}
	\label{fig:linear}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Quadratic Transformation}

For $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $y = x^2$ ($y \geq 0$), there are two inverses: $x_1(y) = \sqrt{y}$ and $x_2(y) = -\sqrt{y}$. The Jacobian is $|dx/dy| = 1/(2\sqrt{y})$. Applying the formula:
\begin{align}
	p(y) & = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{y}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} \nonumber \\
	     & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}\exp\left(-\frac{y}{2}\right), \quad y \geq 0
\end{align}

We verify this in Figure~\ref{fig:quadratic}.

\begin{figure}[h] % Single column figure
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/quadratic_transform_verification.png}
	\caption{Verification of quadratic transformation $y = x^2$. Sample mean $\approx 1.0$ and variance $\approx 2.0$}
	\label{fig:quadratic}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Sinusoidal Transformation}

For $x \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 2\pi)$ and $y = \sin(x)$, the equation $\sin(x) = y$ has two solutions:

\begin{equation}
	x_1 = \sin^{-1}(y), \quad x_2 = \pi - \sin^{-1}(y)
\end{equation}

The Jacobian is:
\begin{equation}
	\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| = \frac{1}{|\cos(x)|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}
\end{equation}

Applying the multi-valued transformation formula with $p(x) = 1/(2\pi)$:
\begin{align}
	p(y) & = 2 \times \frac{1}{2\pi} \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^2}} \nonumber \\
	     & = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-y^2}}, \quad y \in (-1, 1)
\end{align}

This is the \textbf{arcsine distribution}, which has infinite density at $y = \pm 1$. Physically, a sinusoid spends more time near its peaks and troughs (where the rate of change is slowest) than near zero (where the rate of change is fastest). Figure~\ref{fig:sin} verifies this result.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/sin_transform_arcsine.png}
	\caption{Sinusoidal transformation $y = \sin(x)$ where $x \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 2\pi)$. The histogram matches the theoretical arcsine distribution.}
	\label{fig:sin}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Clipped Sinusoid}

Consider the clipped sinusoid $y = \min(\sin(x), 0.7)$, an idealised model of electronic saturation or clipping. The transformation has two regions:

\textbf{Region 1: No clipping} ($\sin(x) < 0.7$):
\begin{equation}
	p(y) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-y^2}}, \quad -1 < y < 0.7
\end{equation}

\textbf{Region 2: Clipping} ($\sin(x) \geq 0.7$):
All $x$ values where $\sin(x) \geq 0.7$ map to $y = 0.7$, creating a point mass. The clipped interval $[\sin^{-1}(0.7), \pi - \sin^{-1}(0.7)]$ has length $\pi - 2\sin^{-1}(0.7)$, giving:
\begin{equation}
	P(y = 0.7) = \frac{\pi - 2\sin^{-1}(0.7)}{2\pi} \approx 0.253
\end{equation}

The complete PDF is:
\begin{equation}
	p(y) = \begin{cases}
		\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-y^2}}   & -1 < y < 0.7 \\
		\delta(y - 0.7) \cdot 0.253 & y = 0.7
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}

When $\sin(x) = 0.7$, we have $|dy/dx| = |\cos(x)| \to \sqrt{1-0.7^2} \approx 0.71$. However, the key insight is that a finite range of $x$ values (approximately 25.3\% of the input domain) all map to the single output value $y = 0.7$, concentrating probability mass into a delta function. Figure~\ref{fig:clipped} shows the characteristic spike at $y = 0.7$.

\begin{figure}[h]

	\caption{Clipped sinusoid $y = \min(\sin(x), 0.7)$. The spike at $y = 0.7$ represents approximately 25.3\% of samples where saturation occurs.}

\end{figure}

\section{Inverse CDF Method}
\label{sec:inverse_cdf}

The inverse CDF method provides a universal framework for generating samples from arbitrary distributions by transforming uniform random variables.

\subsection{Theory}

If $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and $Y = F^{-1}(U)$ where $F$ is any CDF, then $Y$ has distribution with CDF $F$.

\textbf{Proof:} For any $y$,
\begin{align}
	\Pr(Y \leq y) & = \Pr(F^{-1}(U) \leq y) \nonumber \\
	              & = \Pr(U \leq F(y)) \nonumber      \\
	              & = F(y)
\end{align}
where the last step uses $\Pr(U \leq u) = u$ for $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.

\textbf{Algorithm:}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Generate $u^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$
	\item Compute $y^{(i)} = F^{-1}(u^{(i)})$
	\item Then $y^{(i)}$ has distribution with CDF $F$
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Exponential Distribution}

For the exponential distribution with PDF $p(y) = \exp(-y)$ for $y \geq 0$:

\textbf{CDF:}
\begin{equation}
	F(y) = \int_{0}^{y} \exp(-t)\, dt = 1 - \exp(-y)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Inverse CDF:} Solving $u = F(y)$ for $y$:
\begin{align}
	y & = -\ln(1 - u)
\end{align}
Thus $F^{-1}(u) = -\ln(1 - u)$ for $u \in (0,1)$. Since $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ implies $(1-U) \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$, we can generate samples using $Y = -\ln(U)$ instead.

\subsection{Implementation}

\begin{verbatim}
def generate_exponential(n, mean=1.0):
    u = np.random.rand(n)
    return -mean * np.log(u)
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Monte Carlo Estimation}

Given $N$ i.i.d.\ samples $y^{(1)}, \ldots, y^{(N)}$ from a distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, we define the sample mean estimator:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} y^{(i)}
\end{equation}

For the exponential distribution with $\lambda = 1$, the theoretical mean is $E[Y] = 1$ and variance is $\text{Var}(Y) = 1$. Experimental verification with $N = 10000$ samples yields $\hat{\mu} = 1.021$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2 = 1.025$, confirming the inverse CDF method correctly generates exponential variates.

\subsection{Bias of the Mean Estimator}

The sample mean $\hat{\mu}$ is an unbiased estimator of the true mean $\mu$. To prove this, we compute the expectation over all possible samples:
\begin{align}
	E[\hat{\mu}] & = E\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} y^{(i)}\right] \nonumber \\
	             & = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} E[y^{(i)}] \nonumber            \\
	             & = \frac{1}{N} \cdot N \cdot \mu = \mu
\end{align}
where we used linearity of expectation and that each $y^{(i)}$ has the same mean $\mu$.

\subsection{Variance of the Mean Estimator}

The variance of $\hat{\mu}$ determines how quickly the estimate converges to the true mean:
\begin{align}
	\text{Var}(\hat{\mu}) & = \text{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} y^{(i)}\right) \nonumber    \\
	                      & = \frac{1}{N^2} \text{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} y^{(i)}\right) \nonumber \\
	                      & = \frac{1}{N^2} \cdot N\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}
\end{align}
where we used independence of samples (variance of sum equals sum of variances).

The standard error is $\text{SE}(\hat{\mu}) = \sigma/\sqrt{N}$, implying that doubling the number of samples only reduces the error by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$. This $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ convergence rate is fundamental to Monte Carlo methods.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Convergence}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\singlecolwidth]{img/mc_convergence_single.png}\\[0.5em]
	\includegraphics[width=\singlecolwidth]{img/mc_convergence_avg.png}
	\caption{Monte Carlo convergence for exponential distribution. Top: Single run showing squared error $(\hat{\mu} - \mu)^2$ versus $N$ with theoretical variance $\sigma^2/N$ (dashed). Bottom: Averaged squared error over 100 runs confirms $1/N$ decay rate.}
	\label{fig:mc_convergence}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mc_convergence} demonstrates the $1/N$ variance decay. While individual runs show fluctuations, the averaged squared error closely follows the theoretical $\sigma^2/N$ bound. This confirms that the Monte Carlo estimator is consistent: as $N \to \infty$, $\hat{\mu} \to \mu$ by the Law of Large Numbers.

\section{Alpha-Stable Distribution}
\label{sec:stable}

The $\alpha$-stable family generalizes the Gaussian distribution with power-law tails, providing a model for heavy-tailed phenomena in communications, finance, and signal processing.

\subsection{Algorithm}

To generate samples with $\alpha \in (0,2)$, $\alpha \neq 1$ and $\beta \in [-1,+1]$:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Calculate auxiliary parameters:
	      \begin{align}
		      b & = \frac{1}{\alpha}\tan^{-1}\left(\beta\tan\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right) \\
		      s & = \left(1 + \beta^2\tan^2\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)^{1/(2\alpha)}
	      \end{align}

	\item Generate $U \sim \mathcal{U}(-\pi/2, +\pi/2)$ and $V \sim \mathcal{E}(1)$

	\item Calculate:
	      \begin{equation}
		      X = s \frac{\sin(\alpha(U + b))}{(\cos U)^{1/\alpha}}\left(\frac{\cos(U - \alpha(U + b))}{V}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}
	      \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementation}

\begin{verbatim}
def generate_stable(alpha, beta, n):
    b = (1/alpha) * np.arctan(
        beta * np.tan(np.pi*alpha/2))
    s = (1 + beta**2 * np.tan(
        np.pi*alpha/2)**2)**(1/(2*alpha))
    U = np.random.uniform(
        -np.pi/2, np.pi/2, n)
    V = np.random.exponential(1.0, n)
    num = np.sin(alpha*(U+b)) / \
          np.cos(U)**(1/alpha)
    den = (np.cos(U-alpha*(U+b))/V) \
          **((1-alpha)/alpha)
    return s * num * den
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Parameter Interpretation}

\textbf{Stability parameter $\alpha \in (0,2)$:} Controls tail heaviness. At $\alpha = 0.5$, tails are extremely heavy with massive outliers. At $\alpha = 1.5$, tails are lighter but still heavier than Gaussian. As $\alpha \to 2$, the distribution approaches Gaussian.

\textbf{Skewness parameter $\beta \in [-1,+1]$:} Controls asymmetry. At $\beta = 0$, the distribution is symmetric. Positive $\beta$ produces right skewness; negative $\beta$ produces left skewness. Figure~\ref{fig:stable} illustrates these effects for different $\alpha$ and $\beta$ combinations.

\subsection{Tail Probability Analysis}

A key distinction between $\alpha$-stable and Gaussian distributions lies in their tail behaviour. For $\alpha$-stable distributions with $\alpha < 2$, the tail probability follows a power law:
\begin{equation}
	P(|X| > t) \sim c \cdot t^{-\alpha} \quad \text{as } t \to \infty
\end{equation}

In contrast, the Gaussian has exponentially decaying tails: $P(|Z| > t) \sim e^{-t^2/2}/(t\sqrt{\pi})$.

Table~\ref{tab:tail_probs} demonstrates the dramatic difference: at $t = 6$, the Gaussian tail probability is essentially zero ($\sim 10^{-9}$), while the $\alpha$-stable with $\alpha = 0.5$ still has $P(|X| > 6) \approx 0.21$. This explains why heavy-tailed distributions produce extreme outliers.

Figure~\ref{fig:tail_fit} confirms that $P(|X| > t) \propto t^{\gamma}$ with $\gamma \approx -\alpha$. Linear regression on the log-log plot yields $\gamma = -0.50$ for $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\gamma = -1.50$ for $\alpha = 1.5$, validating the general formula $\gamma = -\alpha$.

\subsection{Convergence to Gaussian}

As the stability parameter $\alpha$ approaches 2, the $\alpha$-stable distribution converges to a Gaussian. This transition involves fundamental changes in the distribution's properties:

\textbf{Tail behaviour:} For $\alpha < 2$, tails follow a power law with infinite variance. At $\alpha = 2$, tails decay exponentially with finite variance.

\textbf{Characteristic function:} The $\alpha$-stable characteristic function is $\phi(t) = \exp(-|t|^\alpha)$. As $\alpha \to 2$, this approaches $\exp(-t^2)$, the Gaussian characteristic function.

\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:stable_convergence} illustrates this convergence visually. At $\alpha = 1.99$, the histogram is nearly indistinguishable from the Gaussian reference, while at $\alpha = 0.5$ the heavy tails produce a sharply peaked distribution with frequent extreme values.

\section{Conclusion}

This work develops a comprehensive analysis on random variables. Beginning with histogram analysis, multinomial distribution theory reveals that estimation uncertainty scales as $\sqrt{J/N}$ for uniform distributions but diverges in low-probability regions, motivating kernel density estimation with its superior convergence rates. The Jacobian transformation formula then shows how functional mappings reshape probability distributions. The inverse CDF method provides universal sampling from arbitrary distributions by inverting the transformation $U = F(Y)$ for uniform $U$.

The practical limitations of these techniques emerge through variance analysis and tail behavior. Monte Carlo estimation exhibits fundamental $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ convergence, meaning quadrupled sample sizes merely halve estimation error regardless of algorithmic sophistication. While Gaussian tails decay exponentially as $\exp(-t^2/2)$, $\alpha$-stable tails follow power laws $t^{-\alpha}$, producing extreme values orders of magnitude more frequently. The stability parameter $\alpha$ directly governs this tail heaviness, with the Gaussian recovered as the limiting case $\alpha \to 2$. These tools collectively enable practitioners to generate, analyze, and understand random phenomena beyond standard distributional assumptions.
